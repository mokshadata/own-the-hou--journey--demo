After your offer is accepted, your lender will order a home appraisal. An <Term term="appraisal" mode="definer">**appraisal**</Term> is an independent estimate of the home’s value, conducted by a licensed appraiser. Its purpose is to protect the lender by making sure the home is worth the amount being borrowed — but it also protects you from overpaying on the house price.

### Paying for the Appraisal

Even though your lender orders the appraisal, as the homebuyer, you will pay for it – and you usually need to pay for it before the closing. The lender will let you know what the cost is before ordering the appraisal. The cost varies, but as of 2026, it’s typically around $400-$500 for a mid-size home.

## How an Appraisal Works

The appraiser examines the home and determines its <Term term="appraised value" mode="definer">**appraised value**</Term> by:

* Assessing the size, condition, and features of the property
* Noting any visible issues that may affect value
* Reviewing recent sales of similar homes in the area

The appraiser does not do a full inspection and does not enter the home — that’s why inspections and appraisals serve different purposes.

### What If the Appraisal Is Too Low?

If the appraisal comes in **at or above the purchase price**, the loan usually moves forward without issue. If the appraisal comes in **lower than the agreed price**, you have several options:

* Renegotiate the price with the seller.
* Ask the seller to cover the difference.
* Bring additional cash to closing to cover the “appraisal gap” – the difference between the appraisal value and the price included in your offer.
* Or, if your contract includes the right protections, you can walk away from the deal.

Your realtor and housing counselor can help you understand which options make sense for your situation.
