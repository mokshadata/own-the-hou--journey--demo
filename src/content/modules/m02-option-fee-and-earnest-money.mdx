When you make an offer on a home, you’ll usually be asked to provide some money upfront. In Texas, two common payments are the option fee and earnest money. These serve different purposes, and it’s important to understand how each one works before you sign an offer.

<Term term="option-fee" mode="definer">**Option Fee**</Term>

The option fee is a small payment that gives YOU the right to cancel your offer during a short period of time after the offer is accepted. This is often called the <Term term="option-period" mode="definer">**option period**</Term>. This is usually about 7-10 days from when you make the offer, and is when you typically schedule inspections and take a closer look at the property.

The option fee is usually a small, non-refundable payment paid directly to the seller. It is typically around $200, but may be $100-$500. If you decide not to move forward during the option period for any reason, you can cancel the contract  and receive your earnest money back— but you will not get the option fee back. Think of it as the cost of buying time to fully examine the home.

<Term term="earnest-money" mode="definer">**Earnest Money**</Term>

Earnest money is a larger deposit that shows the seller you’re serious about buying the home. It’s usually held by a neutral third party, like a <Term term="title-company" mode="definer">**title company**</Term>, and applied toward your down payment and closing costs if the sale goes through. Before you finalize your offer, talk carefully with your realtor about the terms related to earnest money.

* **Make sure you understand when you can get your earnest money back — and when you can’t.**  In many cases, you can get your earnest money back if you don’t buy the home, as long as the contract includes the right protections. If the deal falls through due to issues like a low appraisal, a failed inspection, or problems with mortgage approval, you may be able to get this money back. However, in competitive markets, sellers may push buyers to waive certain protections. For example, you might be asked to give up the right to get your earnest money back if the appraisal comes in low or inspections reveal problems. These decisions can carry real financial risk. Your realtor is your number one advocate in these negotiations.
* **Make sure you know how much money you need in the bank to cover the option fee and earnest money requirements.** Earnest money is a part of your down payment and closing costs. By the time you make an offer, you should have worked with your lender and realtor to plan for the cash you will need to cover these costs. However, it’s important to make sure this money is accessible – likely in a savings or checking account – when you need it. Also, make sure not to make any big purchases that could cause you to fall short once you make an offer, because this could make the entire deal fall through.
